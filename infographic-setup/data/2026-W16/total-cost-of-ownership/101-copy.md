## LinkedIn Caption

Why did your company choose the cheapest supplier and end up spending more?

This is Episode 15 of Supply Chain 101. In Episode 14 we covered how to classify SKUs by value and demand pattern. Today we're looking at how companies evaluate what they pay for those SKUs, because the number on the purchase order is only the tip of the iceberg.

Total Cost of Ownership (TCO) is what you actually pay when you account for everything beyond unit price.

Think of it as an iceberg:
Above the waterline is the unit price. It's visible, it's on the quote, and it's what most procurement negotiations focus on.

Below the waterline is everything else:
- Transport and customs: freight, duties, insurance
- Quality costs: inspections, rejects, rework, returns
- Inventory carrying: safety stock buffers driven by lead time variability
- Administrative overhead: supplier management, communication, expediting
- Lead time costs: the cash tied up while goods are in transit or in buffer
- Payment terms: the financial impact of when you actually pay

In most cases, the below-waterline costs are 2-3x larger than the unit price itself. And they're spread across different departments (logistics, quality, finance, planning) so nobody connects them back to the original sourcing decision.

What often happens is in practice the supplier that's 15% cheaper on unit price is picked but has 3x longer lead times requires significantly more safety stock. That inventory costs money to hold, ties up working capital, and carries obsolescence risk. Run the full TCO calculation and the saving often disappears.

Does your procurement team evaluate on unit price or total cost?

3. [Stat-Lead]: "Unit price is 30-50% of what you actually pay for a product. The rest is invisible: freight, quality failures, payment terms, lead time buffers, inventory holding, admin overhead. That total is called Total Cost of Ownership, and most procurement decisions ignore it."
4. [Contrarian]: "The cheapest supplier on paper is often the most expensive supplier in practice. But procurement teams keep choosing on unit price because it's the number on the quote, and the hidden costs don't show up until months later in someone else's budget."
5. [Paradox]: "You negotiated a 15% lower unit price. Your total cost went up. How? Because the cheaper supplier had 3x longer lead times, which required 3x more safety stock, which tied up cash, which erased the saving and then some. The price went down. The cost went up."
6. [Personal-Reflection]: "I've seen supplier decisions where the unit price savings were real and measurable on day one, but within six months the quality failures, expedited freight charges, and inventory carrying costs had quietly eaten the entire difference. The procurement team hit their target. The P&L didn't."
7. [Result-First]: "Quality failures that require rework. Expedited freight to cover late deliveries. Safety stock buffers for unreliable lead times. Admin hours managing a difficult supplier relationship. None of these appear on the purchase order. All of them appear on the total cost."
8. [Timeline-Shock]: "January: you switch to a supplier that's 15% cheaper per unit. March: the first quality reject comes back. You rework it internally. April: a late delivery forces airfreight to meet a customer deadline. June: your planner increases safety stock by 40% because lead times are inconsistent. December: someone calculates the total cost. The 'cheaper' supplier cost 8% more."
9. [Comparison-Gap]: "Supplier A: higher unit price, reliable lead times, consistent quality, nearby. Supplier B: lower unit price, variable lead times, occasional quality issues, overseas. 10. [Decision-Pressure]: "Does your procurement team evaluate suppliers on unit price or total cost? If the answer is unit price, every hidden cost (freight, quality, inventory, admin) is being absorbed somewhere else in the business without anyone connecting it back to the sourcing decision."
