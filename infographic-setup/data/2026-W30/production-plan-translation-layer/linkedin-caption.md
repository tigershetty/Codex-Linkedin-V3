## Recommended Caption — Voice-QA Pass

The demand plan says what the business wants; the production plan says what the factory can actually build.

That sounds like a small distinction, but in real planning conversations it becomes a very practical problem.

Sales may see demand. Finance may see revenue. Customer service may see a promise date. But operations has to translate that into lines, people, materials, tooling, changeovers, quality checks, supplier arrivals, and all the smaller constraints that do not show up neatly when the number first changes.

That is where the production plan sits.

It is the translation layer between demand ambition and factory commitment. It takes the demand signal and asks a more grounded set of questions:

• Do we have the capacity?
• Do we have the material?
• Do we have the labor and skills?
• What does the sequence do to changeover?
• What gets delayed if we pull this forward?
• Is this still a plan, or has it become a fire drill?

The factory schedule is more detailed. It says what runs on which line, in which sequence, and when.

The production plan comes before that. It decides what the factory should commit to build across the planning horizon after the constraints have been checked.

And this is why the sentence "can you just change the plan?" is rarely as simple as it sounds. Sometimes the answer is yes. Sometimes the answer is yes, but with a service, cost, overtime, inventory, or changeover trade-off. And sometimes the honest answer is no, because the constraint moved faster than the plan could absorb.

My view is that a good production plan does not make the factory rigid. It makes the trade-offs visible early enough for the business to make a better decision.

When demand changes, which constraint changes the plan first in your environment?
