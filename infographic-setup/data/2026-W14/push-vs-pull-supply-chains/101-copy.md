## LinkedIn Caption

Why does IKEA build millions of bookshelves before anyone orders one, while Dell used to wait for your exact configuration before assembling a single laptop?

Both were right. The difference is where each company draws the line.

This is Episode 6 of Supply Chain 101. In Episode 5 we covered S&OP and how companies align sales ambition with operational reality.

Today we're looking at the fundamental design choice underneath all of that: do you make before the customer asks, or after?

- A push supply chain produces based on what it expects to sell. Raw materials are ordered, products are manufactured, and inventory is moved into warehouses and stores before a single customer order exists. The upside is speed to the customer. The risk is overstock, markdowns, and waste when the forecast is wrong.

- A pull supply chain waits for actual demand. Nothing moves until a customer places an order. The upside is that you only build what's been bought. The risk is longer lead times and the possibility that customers won't wait.

But here's what makes this more interesting than a simple either/or: most supply chains are hybrid. They push up to a certain point (raw materials, components, semi-finished goods) and pull from that point forward (final assembly, customisation, delivery).

That boundary is called the decoupling point, and where you place it determines your inventory, your lead time, and your responsiveness.

- Moving the decoupling point upstream makes you more responsive but requires better demand visibility.
- Moving it downstream makes you more efficient but slower to react.

There's no universal right answer, only a right answer for your product, your customer, and your market. Where does the decoupling point sit in your supply chain? And did someone choose it, or did it just end up there?
