## LinkedIn Caption

Why did some companies recover from 2020-2025 disruptions in weeks while others took years?

Not because they predicted the events. Because they had categorised the risks, and the framework told them where to look first.

This is Episode 21 of Supply Chain 101. In Episode 20 we covered sourcing strategy and the trade-offs between single, dual, and multi-source. Today we're zooming out to look at the broader question: how do you systematically think about everything that could go wrong, without trying to predict the unpredictable?

The starting point is a 2x2 risk matrix: probability on one axis, impact on the other. Every potential disruption gets plotted.

Within that matrix, supply chain risks tend to fall into five categories:
- Geopolitical: trade wars, sanctions, regional conflicts, regulatory changes
- Supplier: financial collapse, capacity loss, quality failure, single-point-of-failure
- Demand: sudden shifts, customer concentration, market collapse
- Operational: cyberattacks, IT failures, labour disruption, internal capacity loss
- External: pandemics, climate events, infrastructure failures, currency shocks

Here's what's important to remember: you don't need to predict the next pandemic, the next ship-stuck-in-a-canal, or the next regional conflict. You need to know which of these five categories your operation is most exposed to right now, and have a documented response for each. The framework isn't a crystal ball. It's a map of where to look first when something changes.

The two levers that consistently separate companies that recover from those that struggle are visibility (do you know what's happening upstream and downstream as it unfolds?) and optionality (do you have alternatives ready, or do you have to scramble to find them?).

Does your supply chain have a documented risk register?

## Hook Options — All 10 (reference)
1. [Question-Why]: "Why did some companies recover from 2020-2025 disruptions in weeks while others took years? Not because they predicted the events. Because they had categorised the risks, and the framework told them where to look first."
2. [Question-How]: "How do you build a supply chain risk framework when leadership wants one in 30 days and your team has been firefighting for the past three years? You start with five categories, plot them on a 2x2, and accept that not knowing exactly what will happen is fine, as long as you know which type of disruption you're vulnerable to."
3. [Stat-Lead]: "Every supply chain disruption between 2020 and 2025 was predictable. Not the specific event, but the category. Companies that survived had risk frameworks. Companies that scrambled had spreadsheets, and they're not the same thing."
4. [Contrarian]: "Risk management in supply chain isn't about preventing disruption. That's impossible. It's about reducing recovery time. The companies that did this best in the last five years didn't predict events better than anyone else. They just had two levers ready: visibility into where they were exposed, and optionality in how they could respond."
5. [Paradox]: "You can't predict what specific disruption will hit your supply chain next year. But you can predict which categories of disruption you're most exposed to right now. The risk framework doesn't tell you the future. It tells you where to look when something changes."
6. [Personal-Reflection]: "I've watched leadership teams panic-build risk registers in the middle of disruptions. The exercise is rarely useful in the moment because the framework wasn't there before things broke. Risk management is one of those things that has zero visible value until the day you need it, and then it has all the value at once."
7. [Result-First]: "A supplier collapses. Your team responds in 48 hours because you'd already qualified an alternative. A geopolitical conflict cuts off a region. You shift volume to a different geography because the framework told you to spread it. Both are risk management working. Neither feels like crisis management, which is the point."
8. [Timeline-Shock]: "2020: pandemic disrupts supply. 2021: Suez Canal blockage. 2022: war in Europe. 2023: semiconductor shortages. 2024: Red Sea shipping disruptions. 2025: more of the same. Five years, five categories of disruption, all predictable in type if not in timing. Companies with frameworks adapted. Companies without them rebuilt every time."
9. [Comparison-Gap]: "A risk register is a list. A risk framework is a structure. One catalogues what could go wrong. The other tells you what to do about it. Most companies have the first and call it the second, and that's why their risk management feels like documentation rather than capability."
10. [Decision-Pressure]: "Does your supply chain have a documented risk register? And more importantly, when was it last reviewed against actual events that affected your operation?"
