A freight saving can be real while the decision to wait is still indefensible.

Picture the moment: one PO is ready, a second compatible PO may be ready soon, and the combined
shipment looks cheaper. It is very easy to turn that into a freight calculation, because the
saving is the number everyone can see.

The more useful question comes before it: does the customer need-by date still give us legitimate
room to wait?

The working order is straightforward, although the inputs rarely are:

1. Check that the shipments are genuinely compatible and that the key inputs are current.
2. Test the customer date against credible supplier-ready and transit scenarios — not one optimistic
   promised date.
3. Only when the date is protected does it make sense to compare the current freight opportunity.

If one credible waiting scenario misses the customer need-by while shipping now protects it, the
standard decision is to ship now. The freight saving may still exist, but it is no longer a clean
decision to act on.

There can be exceptions, of course. In that case the KAM and Purchasing Manager should escalate
together, record the risk, and make the trade-off visible. Approval does not turn the risk into a
safe service outcome — and if this keeps happening, the underlying rule, data, supplier promise, or
customer-date process needs attention.

Essentially, consolidation is not a rule to follow or avoid. It is a decision that has to earn its
permission from the customer-safe window first.
