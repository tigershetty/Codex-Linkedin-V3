# Direction 02 — One Shipment, Two Valid Scorecards

**Creative family:** split-perspective decision board  
**Role:** leadership, KAM, and purchasing conversation candidate  
**Content mode:** proposed cross-functional decision framework

## First-frame hook

> **ONE SHIPMENT.**  
> **TWO VALID SCORECARDS.**  
> **ONE DECISION THEY HAVE TO MAKE TOGETHER.**

The hook acknowledges the legitimate commercial tension instead of casting Purchasing or KAM as the problem.

## Reader moment

The Purchasing Manager sees a viable consolidation and a possible freight saving. The KAM sees a customer promise and an uncertain readiness date. Both are working with a real part of the decision; neither view is sufficient on its own.

## Visual argument

Create one central, tactile shipment board with a shared **release lever** that cannot be pulled from either side alone:

- Left perspective: `PURCHASING` with a freight card, compatibility tick, and quote-freshness marker.
- Right perspective: `KAM` with a customer need-by card and a scenario band rather than one certain date.
- Centre: the physical pallet / release gate both perspectives feed.
- Below the gate: three visible outcomes — `SHIP NOW`, `CONSOLIDATE`, `ESCALATE`.
- The centre is deliberately larger than either function: it tells the reader that a customer-safe, economically sound decision is joint work, not a hand-off.

Use distinct semantic colour sparingly: azure for the shared operating system, green for a protected date, coral for an exposed commitment. The visual is a premium operating artefact, not a RACI table, opinion graphic, or two-column fight.

## Story mechanism

The reader discovers that the conflict is not “service versus cost.” It is two partial scorecards being mistaken for a complete decision:

1. Purchasing’s scorecard makes cost visible.
2. KAM’s scorecard makes the customer commitment visible.
3. The shared gate brings timing, compatibility, data freshness, and economics into one release decision.
4. When a credible scenario misses need-by, the exception requires both owners — but approval records the risk; it does not erase it.

## What gets saved

**The shared release rule:**

> Do not ask either function to “own” a service-risk consolidation alone. Purchasing validates the commercial and compatibility side; KAM validates the customer commitment; the exception is jointly escalated when the two do not clear the same gate.

This is an approved-in-meaning Tiger operating recommendation, not evidence of any named organisation's process.

## Proposed short caption opening

> One shipment can look perfectly sensible from two different desks. Purchasing may have a real cost opportunity in front of them, while the KAM has a customer promise that cannot be reduced to a freight comparison — which is why the decision should meet at one shared gate rather than becoming a hand-off.

## Kill criteria

Kill or rework this direction if any of the following is true:

- The split reads as departments arguing rather than two legitimate inputs converging.
- The shared gate and decision outcomes are not immediately legible.
- It slips into generic collaboration language without a specific release decision.
- It suggests KAM or Purchasing can unilaterally make a customer-risk exception safe.
- It becomes a flat “roles and responsibilities” diagram with no visual tension or operating object.
