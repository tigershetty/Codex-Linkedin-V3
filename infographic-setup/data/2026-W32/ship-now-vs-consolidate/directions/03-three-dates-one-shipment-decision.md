# Direction 03 — Three Dates, One Shipment Decision

**Creative family:** deadline corridor / visual timing model  
**Role:** highest save-utility candidate  
**Content mode:** practical date-check framework with explicit uncertainty boundary

## First-frame hook

> **THREE DATES.**  
> **ONE SHIPMENT DECISION.**  
> **MISS ONE, AND “CONSOLIDATE” IS ONLY A GUESS.**

The reader should immediately recognise that a freight decision is really a timing decision with three separate dates, not one delivery promise.

## Reader moment

Someone says the second PO will be ready “in a few days.” The freight quote is still valid today. The customer need-by is fixed or needs reconfirmation. Before holding the ready PO, the team needs to see whether those three clocks still overlap.

## Visual argument

Design a single horizontal **time corridor** from today to customer need-by:

- Date 1: `PO A READY` — the starting point, represented by the ready shipment.
- Date 2: `PO B CREDIBLY READY` — shown as a range or band, never a single fabricated promise.
- Date 3: `CUSTOMER NEED-BY` — the hard gate at the far end.
- A moveable `QUOTE VALID UNTIL` tag sits beneath the corridor as an economic boundary rather than a fourth date competing with the core logic.
- The only useful safe zone is the overlap that allows PO B readiness, movement, and delivery before the need-by. A coral break in the corridor makes an exposed path unmistakable.

The composition should feel like a premium physical planning board: one large visual spine, minimal supporting cards, vivid but restrained semantic colours, and enough whitespace to read it from a phone. It must not use invented calendar dates, transit durations, percentages, or a fictional customer.

## Story mechanism

The post turns an ambiguous phrase — “wait a few days” — into a short inspection sequence:

1. What is ready now?
2. What is credibly, not optimistically, expected to be ready?
3. What does the customer actually need, and how clear is that date?
4. Only then: is the freight quote still useful enough to compare?

The payoff is simple: consolidation is possible only inside a real overlap. When the range does not clear the customer gate, stop calling it an optimisation; it is an exception decision.

## What gets saved

**The three-date check:**

1. Ready-now date for the shipment being held.
2. Credible ready range for the shipment being added.
3. Customer need-by date, including its confidence and consequence.

Then check whether the commercial quote is current enough to be compared. This gives practitioners a reusable pre-calculation control without pretending to forecast an exact arrival.

## Proposed short caption opening

> “The second PO should be ready in a few days” sounds like a planning update, but it is not yet a consolidation decision. Before a ready shipment is held back, three dates need to fit together: what is ready now, what is credibly expected next, and what the customer actually needs.

## Kill criteria

Kill or rework this direction if any of the following is true:

- It becomes a generic lead-time explainer rather than a release decision for a ready PO.
- The reader cannot name the three dates from the first frame without reading fine print.
- A single clean date hides the uncertainty that is the point of the post.
- Freight cost dominates the image and makes the customer need-by look secondary.
- The corridor becomes an over-engineered Gantt chart or relies on fabricated values to feel useful.
