# Direction 01 — Which Order Gets to Wait?

**Creative family:** physical operating scene / decision story  
**Role:** primary reach candidate  
**Content mode:** proposed decision method with a compact practitioner rule

## First-frame hook

> **ONE ORDER IS READY.**  
> **THE OTHER MAKES FREIGHT CHEAPER.**  
> **WHICH ORDER GETS TO WAIT?**

The opening must feel like a loading-dock choice, not an educational poster about freight consolidation.

## Reader moment

One PO is physically ready to leave. A second compatible PO could make the shipment cheaper if it arrives in time. The customer need-by has not disappeared simply because the freight quote looks better.

## Visual argument

Build a bright, premium operations scene around one central **departure gate**:

- Left: a ready pallet marked `READY NOW`.
- Centre: a clean decision gate with two possible movements — `SHIP` and `WAIT`.
- Right: a customer need-by gate, visibly closer than the reader expects.
- A second pallet approaches on a dotted path marked `MAY BE READY` rather than a promised date.
- The visual payoff is the small time window between “ready now” and “latest safe departure.” That window, not the freight saving, determines whether the first pallet may wait.

Use the richer Shetty's Desk V3 language: luminous white field, azure operational system, green only for a protected/customer-safe path, coral only for exposed risk, and tactile 2.5D warehouse objects. Do not depict real company data, a customer, a rate, or a predicted arrival.

## Story mechanism

The reader follows a miniature story:

1. A ready order has a concrete opportunity to leave.
2. The tempting move is to wait for the second order.
3. The customer commitment turns “wait a few days” into a decision with a finite allowance.
4. If any credible waiting scenario crosses the need-by gate, ship-now is the standard result; an exception remains visible as a separate governance route.

The visual carries the tension. The caption explains the boundary without repeating the whole image.

## What gets saved

**The Wait Permission rule:**

> A ready order can wait only inside the customer-safe window — after compatibility and input freshness are checked. If a credible waiting scenario misses need-by while ship-now protects it, ship now.

This is intentionally a decision distinction, not a hard-coded formula or a claim that every organisation should use the same buffer.

## Proposed short caption opening

> The awkward part of consolidation is that the saving can be real while the wait is still not defensible. When one PO is ready and the next one is nearly ready, the first question is not how much freight we can save; it is whether the customer date still gives us permission to wait.

## Kill criteria

Kill or rework this direction if any of the following is true:

- The first frame reads as “consolidate freight to save money” rather than a customer-time conflict.
- The visual needs more than one sentence to explain the basic choice.
- The central gate is decorative rather than showing how the customer need-by controls the move.
- The scene implies a real outcome, amount, date, customer, or forecast certainty.
- The final image becomes a complicated calculator interface, generic route diagram, or a blue technical blueprint.
