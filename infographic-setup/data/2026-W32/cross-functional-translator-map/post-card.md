**Reader and real work moment:** An early- or mid-career supply-chain practitioner is deciding what
to learn next. They repeatedly hear that they need to become “cross-functional,” but that advice
does not tell them which adjacent function will make their own work more useful.

**Opening tension:** “Get cross-functional exposure” is good advice that is almost useless until
someone tells you which language to learn next.

**The visible proof:** A tactile, fold-out translator map makes six supply-chain languages visibly
adjacent rather than an undifferentiated career ladder. Each starting function points to the next
working language that changes the quality of its decisions: buying → demand signal; planning →
capacity constraint; manufacturing → delivery promise; logistics → customer commitment; analytics
→ operator decision; strategy → frontline workflow.

**The useful keep:** Do not try to become fluent in all of supply chain at once. Locate your
current function, learn the language of the next decision your work affects, and use that link to
choose a project, mentor, rotation, or next role.

**Three rough routes:**

- **A — Adjacent Capability Compass:** A reader starts at their home function on a compass and
  follows one adjacent capability outward. Hook: “Your next supply-chain move may be sideways.”
  Keep: one next capability to deliberately pursue.
- **B — Translator Map (selected):** A fold-out field map shows six functions as working
  languages, linked by the decision each must understand next. Hook: “The people who become
  cross-functional learn one language beyond their own.” Keep: choose the next language by the
  decision your work hands to someone else.
- **C — Handoff Test:** A connected decision chain makes the downstream consequence of every
  function visible. Hook: “You do not become cross-functional by attending more meetings.” Keep:
  trace your work to its next operating constraint.

**Selected route and why:** B has the clearest complete translation: it retains the broad,
identity-relevant navigation mechanism that earned the career-map outlier, but turns a fuzzy piece
of professional advice into one concrete next action. A is too close to a generic career map; C is
more operational but less immediately relatable to the broad audience this calibration needs.

**Visual family:** illustrated editorial artifact — one tactile fold-out working map, not a default
isometric scene, dashboard, card grid, or historical brand kit.

**At feed size, the reader sees:** “Learn one function beyond your own,” then an intriguing map of
six connected operating languages.

**The image makes this claim legible by showing:** no function is an island; every function hands a
decision to another function, so the useful adjacent language is concrete rather than aspirational.

**The useful object or action is:** a one-screen working map readers can use to identify their
next project, mentor, rotation, or role conversation.

**The eye moves from -> through -> to:** headline -> current-function tiles and connecting paths
-> the single next-language rule.

**The caption adds, rather than repeats:** why “cross-functional” often fails as advice, how the
six hand-offs work in practice, and a question about the next language a reader is choosing.
