## LinkedIn Caption

Just-in-Time was the greatest supply chain innovation of the 20th century. Just-in-Case was the lesson of 2020.

This is Episode 19 of Supply Chain 101. In Episode 18 we covered the nearshoring decision and total landed cost. Today we're looking at one of the most misunderstood debates in supply chain: should you run lean or should you hold buffer?

- Just-in-Time (JIT) is an inventory philosophy that originated at Toyota. The idea is simple: materials arrive exactly when they're needed in production, not before. The benefit is minimal waste, lower inventory costs, and a faster, more responsive operation. But it requires extremely reliable suppliers, consistent quality, short replenishment cycles, and stable demand. When those conditions exist, JIT is powerful. When they don't, it's fragile.

- Just-in-Case (JIC) is the opposite instinct: hold enough inventory to absorb disruption. The benefit is resilience. The cost is tied-up capital, warehouse space, and the risk of obsolescence. After 2020, many companies swung hard toward JIC, adding weeks or months of buffer stock across their entire portfolio.

But here's what makes this more nuanced than a binary choice: the right approach depends on the product.

- A high-value, critical component with a single supplier in a volatile region probably needs a JIC buffer.
- A low-cost, widely available commodity with three local suppliers can run JIT safely.

My view on this is that the mature answer is portfolio segmentation. Not every product line needs the same inventory strategy. The companies that segment, applying JIT where conditions support it and JIC where the risk demands it, are managing cost and resilience simultaneously rather than trading one for the other.

Which items in your supply chain run JIT by default rather than by design?

## Hook Options — All 10 (reference)
1. [Question-Why]: "Why did the companies that ran the leanest supply chains before 2020 suffer some of the worst disruptions during it? Because Just-in-Time was designed for a world with reliable suppliers, short distances, and predictable demand. That world changed."
2. [Question-How]: "How does a hospital decide to stockpile six months of PPE while a car manufacturer keeps only four hours of parts on the production line? Both are rational decisions. The difference is how much disruption risk each one is willing to absorb."
3. [Stat-Lead]: "Just-in-Time was the greatest supply chain innovation of the 20th century. Just-in-Case was the lesson of 2020. The answer isn't choosing one. It's knowing which to apply where, and most companies haven't segmented their portfolio to make that decision."
4. [Contrarian]: "JIT didn't fail during COVID. The companies that copied Toyota's low inventory without building Toyota's enabling conditions failed. JIT requires deep supplier partnerships, geographic proximity, and process reliability. Most companies adopted the inventory policy and skipped the prerequisites."
5. [Paradox]: "Toyota invented Just-in-Time to eliminate waste. Then a pandemic, a ship stuck in a canal, and a semiconductor shortage made the world rethink whether that waste was actually a buffer. JIT and JIC are both right. The question is which items in your portfolio need which approach."
6. [Personal-Reflection]: "I've worked in environments that ran JIT by default rather than by design. Nobody had asked whether each product line actually met the conditions for lean inventory. Some did. Some didn't. And the ones that didn't were the first to break when supply got disrupted."
7. [Result-First]: "Production lines stopped. Customer orders went unfulfilled. Companies scrambled to source from anywhere at any price. That was 2020 for JIT supply chains. But the answer wasn't to abandon JIT. It was to stop applying it uniformly across every product, every supplier, and every market."
8. [Timeline-Shock]: "2019: your factory runs with 4 hours of parts on the line. Efficiency is celebrated. 2020: a supplier shuts down. You have 4 hours before the line stops. 2021: leadership mandates 12 weeks of safety stock on everything. Cost spikes. 2023: someone realises the buffer should vary by item, not be a blanket policy. That's the JIT-to-JIC maturity curve in four years."
9. [Comparison-Gap]: "JIT minimises inventory to reduce waste. JIC maximises inventory to reduce risk. One optimises for efficiency. The other optimises for resilience. The mature answer is neither. It's segmenting your portfolio so each product gets the strategy its risk profile actually requires."
10. [Decision-Pressure]: "Which items in your supply chain run Just-in-Time by default rather than by design? If nobody has asked that question, you're carrying either too much risk or too much inventory, and you won't know which until something breaks."
