# Research Brief — supply-contract-clauses
**Pipeline**: 101 · **Week**: 2026-W27 · **Generated**: 2026-06-28
**Standard**: consultant-grade, verified, reliability-tagged

## 1. Framing

The one idea: **A supply contract is not boilerplate — six clauses carry the real risk, and the clause that bites is rarely the one you negotiated hardest.**

Coffee-clarity version: Negotiating teams burn most of their energy on the "what if it all goes wrong" clauses — limitation of liability, indemnities. But the disputes that actually land aren't about those. They're about whether both sides agreed what was being delivered (scope), whether it was accepted, and whether you can leave cleanly. The clause that bites is the one nobody fought over.

Why it matters for a SC audience: a contract is the operating manual for a supplier relationship. When something goes wrong — a late shipment, a quality reject, a price hike, a messy exit — you don't argue from goodwill, you argue from the clause. Knowing which six clauses do the load-bearing work (and which one is quietly under-protected) is the difference between a clean resolution and an expensive one. This post is **clause anatomy** — what each clause does and what bites if it's weak — not pricing-structure mechanics (that's the sister post this week: fixed-price vs cost-plus vs T&M).

### THE SIX LOAD-BEARING CLAUSES — function + what bites

**1. Scope & specification** `CARD-READY`
- Function (plain): defines exactly what is being bought — the goods/services, quantities, specs, and where one party's responsibility ends and the other's begins.
- What bites if weak: scope ambiguity is the **single largest driver of supply-contract disputes (~77% cite scope/goal change; responsibilities ~55%)**. If "what good looks like" isn't pinned, every later argument — late, defective, out-of-spec — has no fixed reference point. Scope ranks #1 on the "most important" list yet is routinely under-specified.
- SOURCE: WorldCC/IACCM survey (scope 77%, responsibilities 55%); Buddle Findlay / Lexology, "The problem of scope in commercial contracts." [Med].

**2. Price & indexation** `CARD-READY` (keep LIGHT — pricing-structure mechanics live in the sister post)
- Function (plain): a price-adjustment / indexation clause lets price move over the contract's life, usually tied to an external index (CPI, or a commodity PPI like steel/fuel), rather than staying fixed.
- What bites if weak: **with no indexation clause, the supplier absorbs inflation and rising input costs** — which under a long fixed price quietly erodes margin until the supplier cuts corners, re-opens the deal, or walks. With an *uncapped* clause, the buyer absorbs the shock. The fix is a transparent public index plus an annual cap (e.g. "indexation capped at X% per year").
- SOURCE: Travers Smith, "Linking prices to inflation: a short guide to indexation clauses"; TLT LLP, "Price adjustment in commercial contracts"; Icertis. [High] (law-firm guidance).

**3. Delivery & lead time (incl. Incoterms)** `CARD-READY`
- Function (plain): sets when/where goods are delivered and, in cross-border trade, which **Incoterms 2020** rule applies — fixing the exact point where **risk of loss and cost transfer** from seller to buyer.
- Key anchor: **Incoterms 2020 = 11 ICC rules**, from **EXW** (least seller responsibility) to **DDP** (most seller responsibility). Each rule answers: when does risk pass, who pays which cost, who handles the paperwork.
- What bites if weak: a vague delivery term (or the wrong Incoterm) means **goods can be damaged or lost in transit and nobody has clearly agreed who carries it** — you discover the risk-transfer point only after the loss. "Acceptance and delivery" is reported as the **#1 issue in contract claims/disputes**.
- SOURCE: ICC, *Incoterms 2020* (11 rules; tasks, costs, risks; risk-transfer point) — primary standards body; WorldCC/IACCM (delivery/acceptance #1 dispute issue). [High] for Incoterms; [Med] for the dispute-ranking.

**4. Quality & SLA (service levels, acceptance, warranties)** `CARD-READY`
- Function (plain): defines the standard the goods/services must hit (specs, **warranties**), the ongoing performance bar for services (**SLAs / service levels**), and the **acceptance** test that says "this conforms — we'll pay."
- What bites if weak:
  - **Deemed/weak acceptance:** under quality-assurance norms, the **risk of loss for nonconforming goods stays with the supplier until cure or acceptance** — so a sloppy or automatic ("deemed") acceptance clause can hand that risk to the buyer prematurely. (FAR 46.5 / UCC Art. 2 framing.)
  - **Warranties:** the UCC lets sellers *limit express warranties, disclaim implied warranties, and cap remedies* — so a buyer who doesn't lock in express warranties and acceptance rights may have **no usable remedy** when goods underperform.
- SOURCE: UCC Article 2 (warranties, remedies — Cornell LII §2-316); FAR Subpart 46.5 Acceptance / 46.7 Warranties; IBM / project44 (SLA function in supply chains). [High] for UCC/FAR; [Med] for SLA practice.

**5. Liability & remedies (limitation of liability, liquidated damages, indemnities)** `CARD-READY` — *this is the over-negotiated one*
- Function (plain): caps how much one party can recover (**limitation of liability**), usually excludes indirect/consequential loss (lost profits); sets pre-agreed sums for defined breaches (**liquidated damages**); and shifts third-party-claim risk (**indemnities**).
- What bites if weak: a low or one-sided liability cap can leave the injured party under-compensated; **liquidated-damages amounts that look like a penalty are unenforceable** (post-*Cavendish v Makdessi* / *ParkingEye* [2015] UKSC 67 — a LD clause must protect a legitimate interest, not impose a disproportionate detriment). BUT — the contrarian point — **this is the cluster teams negotiate hardest (LoL #1, indemnities #2) and it is NOT where most disputes land.** Energy here is real but disproportionate to the actual failure rate.
- SOURCE: Icertis / Sirion / Stevens & Bolton (limitation-of-liability function, consequential-loss exclusion); *Cavendish Square Holding BV v Makdessi*; *ParkingEye Ltd v Beavis* [2015] UKSC 67 (penalty test); WorldCC/IACCM (most-negotiated ranking). [High] for the legal mechanics; [High] for the most-negotiated ranking.

**6. Term & exit (termination, renewal, exit/transition assistance)** `CARD-READY`
- Function (plain): how long the deal runs, how it renews, how either side ends it, and — critically — the **transition/exit-assistance** obligations that let you actually move to a new supplier (data handover, knowledge transfer, staff availability, continuity during the switch).
- What bites if weak: **without contractual exit/transition obligations, your right to terminate is a "paper right"** — you can legally end the contract but switching is so costly and complex you're effectively **locked in** ("vendor lock-in"). Disputes cluster here precisely because contracts say the supplier must give "reasonable assistance" without defining it. Firms "stay with unsuitable suppliers too long due to high switching costs."
- SOURCE: Morgan Lewis ("Transition-Out Obligations in Service Agreements"); Financier Worldwide / Lexology (exit management of outsourcing agreements); Sirion (termination clause). [High] (law-firm/practitioner guidance).

## 3. The "so what" (save-worthy thesis)

**Working hypothesis (term & exit) — partially corrected by the sources.**

The save-worthy thesis the data supports most strongly:

> **The clause that bites is rarely the one you negotiated hardest.** Teams pour negotiation energy into limitation of liability and indemnities — the "if it all blows up" clauses, #1 and #2 most-negotiated since 2007. But the disputes that actually land are about **scope, acceptance/delivery, and responsibilities** — the "did we even agree what this is?" clauses. WorldCC/IACCM's own research shows **only four of the most-negotiated terms even appear on the most-important list.**

So the single most **under-negotiated-relative-to-risk** clause is **Scope & specification** (most important, ~77% dispute driver, chronically under-specified) — with **Delivery/Acceptance** a close second (reported #1 claims/disputes issue).

**On the term-&-exit hypothesis:** it is genuinely under-protected and a real dispute hotspot (the "paper right" / lock-in problem is well-evidenced), so it earns a strong place in the six. But the sources point to **scope and delivery/acceptance**, not exit, as the clauses most disproportionately under-negotiated versus the risk they carry. Recommended framing: lead the thesis on **scope/acceptance** as the clause that bites; keep **exit** as the sharp secondary surprise ("leaving cleanly matters as much as joining — and almost nobody drafts the exit").
