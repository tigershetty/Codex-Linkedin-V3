## Caption (LinkedIn)

Everyone reinforces the wrong clause.

When a supply contract hits the negotiation table, most of the energy goes to one thing: liability. Who pays when something goes wrong. It has been the single most-negotiated term in commercial contracts since 2007, and that makes sense, because nobody wants to be the one left holding the bill.

This is Episode 35 of Supply Chain 101, where I break down one supply chain idea at a time, in plain language, for people inside the field and outside it.

Here is the part most people miss. A supply contract really rests on six clauses, not one:

• Price and indexation: what you pay, and how that price is allowed to move.
• Scope and specification: what you actually agreed to deliver.
• Delivery and lead time: when and how it shows up.
• Quality and SLA: the standard it has to meet.
• Liability and remedies: who carries the risk when it breaks.
• Term and exit: how the relationship starts, and how it ends.

And here is the twist. 77% of contract disputes do not start at liability at all. They start at scope, the clause about what was actually promised, because two parties read the same words and walked away with two different pictures of the job.

So teams armour the clause that rarely cracks, and leave the one that quietly does the most damage half-defined.

My view on this is that a contract is not won in the clause you argue hardest. It is won in the clause you make impossible to misread.
