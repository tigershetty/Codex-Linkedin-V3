# Research Brief — The Flat-Pack Network Decision

**Status:** V4 evidence-led flagship draft

**Claim mode:** `mixed` — documented company case + editorial explainer. No company outcome,
customer result, or universal nearshoring prescription is claimed.

## The public story

IKEA describes its packaging work as making products as tight and flat as possible. Its own
explanation is concrete: a smaller, easier-to-stack box fits more products into a shipment, which
can mean fewer lorries and help keep prices low.

Reuters reported in December 2025 that Inter IKEA planned to source more products from US factories.
Its global supply manager connected the move to a more resilient, responsive supply network and
long lead times for imported furniture in the Americas. The same reporting says US production can
cost more while long-distance shipping has become more costly and unpredictable.

Inter IKEA's FY25 results independently state that trade uncertainty produced higher sourcing costs,
including tariffs, and that transport and logistics costs remained volatile.

## Reader promise

The post does **not** argue that every company should nearshore. It gives a more useful test:

> A product's geometry is part of its network design, so sourcing should be reconsidered SKU by SKU
> when cube, landed cost, duty exposure, volatility, available capacity, and response time stop
> moving together.

## Explicit exclusions

- No old claims about container counts, factory production comparisons, labour premiums, tariff-rate
  stacks, price rises, specific category localisation, or domestic break-even thresholds.
- No use of `data/2026-W08/ikea-re-americanization-flatpack-trap/` as evidence. It is a historical
  draft containing unverified claims and is not a V4 source.
- No use of an IKEA logo, product trade dress, or an imitation of a particular IKEA product in the
  visual.
- No claim that global sourcing is wrong, that nearshoring is always safer, or that the visual
  represents IKEA's exact network.
