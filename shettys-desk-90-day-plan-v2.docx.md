# **Series Logic**

The 24 topics are sequenced so that each infographic builds on the previous one without requiring it. A follower who discovers the series at post 14 can understand that post fully, but a follower who has been reading from post 1 will recognise how the concept connects to foundations already laid.

**Month 1 — The Language:** defines what a supply chain is and how its functions, planning layers, and production strategies relate to each other. After Month 1, the audience has a shared vocabulary.

**Month 2 — The Mechanics:** explains how supply chains buy, hold inventory, manage cost, and move goods. These are the concepts practitioners use daily. After Month 2, the audience understands the operating system.

**Month 3 — The Strategy:** elevates to the decisions that separate average supply chains from competitive advantages. These connect supply chain to business performance. After Month 3, the audience has a complete foundation and a career map.

**Progression within each month:** topics flow from broad to specific. Month 1 starts with the whole system, then zooms into planning types, then production strategies, then the overarching framework (SCOR). Month 2 starts with procurement, moves through inventory management, then cost and logistics models. Month 3 covers demand management, sourcing strategy, risk, financial cycles, and closes with KPIs and careers.

The sequencing follows how a newcomer actually encounters a supply chain: first the whole system, then the functions within it, then how those functions plan and coordinate. No topic requires knowledge from a previous post, but each one adds a layer if followed in order.

# **MONTH 2: THE MECHANICS**

**How supply chains plan inventory, manage cost, and move goods**

Month 2 moves from definitions into the concepts practitioners work with every day. These are the topics where getting it wrong costs real money. Each infographic should feel like a reference card someone pins to their monitor. The sequencing follows the flow of goods: first you buy (procurement), then you hold (inventory), then you manage cost and movement.

| \# | Topic | The Question It Answers | Hook | Visual Format (Single Image) | Caption Direction |
| :---- | :---- | :---- | :---- | :---- | :---- |
| **9** | **Purchasing vs. Procurement** | Are these different roles or just different words for the same thing? | Purchasing buys what someone asked for. Procurement decides whether they should be buying it at all, from whom, and under what terms. One is a transaction. The other is a strategy. | Two-column comparison. Left: Purchasing (reactive, transactional, short-term, cost-focused, PO to payment). Right: Procurement (proactive, strategic, long-term, value-focused, need to supplier management). A small diagram below showing procurement as the larger circle containing purchasing as a subset. | Open with the hook. | **10** | **Direct vs. Indirect Procurement** | We buy raw materials AND office supplies. Same team, same process? | Direct procurement is what goes into your product. | **11** | **The Bullwhip Effect** | Why does my supplier see wild demand swings when my retail sales are stable? | A 5% increase in consumer demand can become a 40% spike at the raw material supplier. Explain that TCO is the real cost of a procurement decision when you account for everything beyond unit price. Give one concrete example: a supplier 15% cheaper on unit price but with 3x longer lead time requires 3x more safety stock, which erases the saving. Close with: does your procurement team evaluate suppliers on unit price or total cost? |
| **16** | **1PL → 2PL → 3PL → 4PL → 5PL** | We use a 3PL. Our competitor uses a 4PL. What are we missing? | Five levels of logistics outsourcing. Each one trades control for capability. # **MONTH 3: THE STRATEGY**

**Where supply chain decisions become business decisions**

Month 3 elevates from mechanics to the decisions that separate average supply chains from competitive advantages. These are the topics that appear in boardroom presentations and consulting engagements. By this point the audience has the vocabulary from months 1 and 2, and each strategic concept connects back to foundations already laid. This month positions Shetty’s Desk as a thought leadership voice, not just an educational one.

| \# | Topic | The Question It Answers | Hook | Visual Format (Single Image) | Caption Direction |

| **17** | **Demand Planning vs. Demand Sensing vs. Demand Shaping** | We forecast demand. But our forecast is always wrong. Now what? | Demand planning predicts what customers will want. Demand sensing detects what they want right now. Demand shaping makes them want what you have. Three capabilities, three time horizons, and most companies only do the first one. | Three horizontal lanes on a timeline. Lane 1: Demand Planning (months ahead, uses history and statistical models, accuracy: 60–75%). Lane 2: Demand Sensing (days to weeks, uses real-time POS, weather, social signals, accuracy: 80–90%). Lane 3: Demand Shaping (proactive, uses pricing, promotions, product substitution to steer demand toward available supply). Each lane has its own colour and a brief capability description. | Open with the hook. Explain that forecasting will never be perfect — the question is what you do about the imperfection. Sensing closes the gap in the short term. Shaping is the most underused: instead of chasing demand, you redirect it. Example: airline pricing is demand shaping. Close with: does your supply chain team have any influence on pricing or promotion decisions, or is that purely a commercial function? |
| **18** | **Nearshoring vs. Offshoring vs. Reshoring** | We manufacture in Asia. Should we move production closer to our market? | 80% of COOs plan to nearshore. 98% have not executed. That 7-factor gap is where the real decision lives. | A world map simplified to three zones with arrows: Offshoring (far, low unit cost, long lead time), Nearshoring (regional, moderate cost, shorter lead time), Reshoring (domestic, high unit cost, lowest total risk). Below: a 7-factor total landed cost checklist (unit cost, freight, duties/tariffs, inventory carrying cost, quality cost, lead time risk, IP risk). Each factor has a directional arrow showing how it shifts across the three strategies. | Open with the 80/98 stat hook. Explain that the unit cost comparison is what kills most nearshoring business cases — it always favours offshoring. But when you add the other 6 factors, the math often flips. Use Zara as the example: nearshored production enabled 10-point gross margin advantage through speed, not cost. Close with: has your organisation run a total landed cost comparison, or are you still looking at unit price? |
| **19** | **Just-in-Time vs. Just-in-Case** | Toyota proved JIT works. Then COVID proved it does not. Who is right? | Just-in-Time was the greatest supply chain innovation of the 20th century. Just-in-Case was the lesson of 2020\. The answer is not one or the other — it is knowing which to apply where. | Two-panel split or balance scale. Left: JIT (minimal inventory, high efficiency, low waste, vulnerable to disruption, Toyota origin). Right: JIC (buffer inventory, higher resilience, higher cost, slower response, post-COVID response). Centre or below: a spectrum showing that most real supply chains sit somewhere between the two, with the position determined by supply risk, demand variability, and product criticality. | Open with the hook. Explain that Toyota’s original JIT was not reckless — it required deep supplier partnerships, geographic proximity, and extreme process reliability. What many Western companies copied was the low-inventory part without the enabling conditions. COVID exposed the gap. The mature answer: segment your portfolio. JIT for stable, low-risk items. JIC for critical, volatile, or single-sourced items. Close with: which items in your portfolio are running JIT by default rather than by design? |
| **20** | **Single Source vs. Dual Source vs. Multi-Source** | We use one supplier. Is that a risk or a strategy? | Single-sourcing gives you leverage through volume. Multi-sourcing gives you resilience through options. The decision between them is not about risk tolerance — it is about which risk you prefer to carry. | Three diagrams side by side. Single source: one line from supplier to company (risk: supply disruption, benefit: deep partnership, volume leverage). Dual source: two lines, primary and backup (risk: split volume reduces leverage, benefit: tested alternative). Multi-source: three or more lines (risk: complexity, higher admin cost, benefit: maximum resilience). Below: a decision matrix with 3 factors (supply risk, spend volume, criticality) and which strategy fits each combination. | Open with the hook. Explain that COVID triggered an overcorrection from single to multi-source without considering the trade-offs. Multi-sourcing a high-volume commodity makes sense. Multi-sourcing a specialised component often increases cost and complexity without improving resilience if all suppliers are in the same geography. Close with: for your top 5 spend categories, can you name a tested alternative supplier? |
| **21** | **Supply Chain Risk Management** | We got disrupted. Now leadership wants a risk framework. Where do we start? | Every supply chain disruption was predictable. Not the specific event — but the category of risk. The companies that survived 2020–2025 had frameworks. The ones that scrambled had spreadsheets. | A 2×2 risk matrix (Probability × Impact) with 5 risk categories plotted: Geopolitical (tariffs, sanctions), Supplier (single source failure, bankruptcy), Demand (sudden shift, bullwhip), Operational (quality failure, capacity constraint), External (climate, pandemic, cyber). Each category is a dot on the matrix with 1–2 real examples from 2020–2025 (Suez Canal, COVID, Hanjin, chip shortage). | Open with the hook. Walk through the 5 categories with one real example each. The reframe: risk management is not about preventing disruption. It is about reducing recovery time. The two levers: visibility (knowing faster) and optionality (having alternatives). Close with: does your supply chain have a documented risk register, or does risk management happen in the moment? |
| **22** | **Order-to-Cash vs. Procure-to-Pay** | Finance keeps mentioning O2C and P2P. How do these connect to supply chain? | O2C is how money comes in. P2P is how money goes out. Supply chain sits in the middle of both. If you do not understand these cycles, you cannot explain your impact to a CFO. | Two horizontal process flows stacked. Top: O2C (Customer PO → Order entry → Fulfilment → Shipping → Invoice → Payment \= DSO). Bottom: P2P (Need identification → Sourcing → PO → Receiving → Invoice matching → Payment \= DPO). Arrows connecting the two flows where supply chain touches both (inventory, warehouse, procurement). The gap between DSO and DPO \= Cash Conversion Cycle. | Open with the hook. Explain that O2C speed determines how fast you collect revenue, P2P efficiency determines how smartly you spend. Supply chain affects both: faster fulfilment shortens DSO, better procurement terms extend DPO. The combination is the cash conversion cycle, which is the metric your CFO actually cares about. Close with: can you calculate your company’s cash conversion cycle? |
| **23** | **The 10 Supply Chain KPIs That Matter** | I measure 50 things. Which ones should I actually report to leadership? | You do not need 50 metrics. You need 10 that map to SCOR’s 5 performance attributes. Two per attribute. One for the boardroom, one for the floor. | A 5-row, 2-column grid. Rows \= SCOR attributes (Reliability, Responsiveness, Agility, Cost, Asset Management). Column 1 \= Strategic KPI (report to CFO/board). Column 2 \= Operational KPI (use on the floor). Examples: Reliability: Perfect Order Rate | On-Time In-Full. Responsiveness: Order Fulfilment Cycle Time | Pick-to-Ship Time. Agility: Upside Supply Chain Flexibility | Supplier Lead Time Variability. Cost: Total Supply Chain Cost as % Revenue | Cost Per Order. Assets: Cash-to-Cash Cycle Time | Inventory Days of Supply. | Open with the hook. Explain that SCOR provides the structure so you are not measuring randomly. The boardroom KPI tells leadership if the supply chain is healthy. The floor KPI tells operators what to fix today. The mistake: reporting operational metrics to the board (they do not care about pick-to-ship time) or giving strategic metrics to the warehouse (they cannot act on cash-to-cash). Close with: which of these 10 does your organisation currently track? |
| **24** | **The Supply Chain Career Map** | I want to grow in supply chain. What are the actual career paths? | Supply chain is not one career. It is at least 6, and they require different skills, different temperaments, and lead to different executive roles. This is the map nobody gives you. | A branching career map starting from Entry Level (analyst, coordinator) and branching into 6 paths: Procurement/Sourcing, Planning/S\&OP, Logistics/Distribution, Manufacturing/Operations, Supply Chain Analytics, and Supply Chain Strategy/Consulting. Each path shows 3 role levels (junior → mid → senior) and the C-suite destination (CPO, CSCO, COO, VP Logistics, etc.). Colour-coded by path. | Open with the hook. This is the series finale. Reference that the previous 23 concepts map to different parts of this career landscape. Planning concepts (posts 3–6) map to the S\&OP path. Procurement concepts (posts 9–10) map to the sourcing path. Inventory concepts (posts 11–14) span planning and analytics. The reframe: the highest-impact supply chain leaders are T-shaped — deep in one path, literate across all six. Close with: which path are you on, and which adjacent path would make you most valuable? |
