## 1. Story in 30 Seconds

IKEA built its global dominance on flat-pack shipping efficiency — a design that lets them move 10x more product per container than assembled-furniture competitors — but that same innovation has made them structurally dependent on Asian manufacturing scale that a US factory at 2 million units per year cannot replicate. When the Supreme Court struck down IEEPA tariffs on February 20, 2026, and Trump immediately imposed a 10-15% global surcharge under Section 122 on top of existing 25% furniture-specific tariffs, IKEA's "absorb and adapt" strategy hit its ceiling: operating profit fell, sofas rose $50, bedroom sets rose $100, and the company publicly admitted it cannot absorb all the costs. The decision a VP Supply Chain faces right now is not whether to localize — it is at what percentage of domestic sourcing the tariff cost exceeds the localization cost premium, and whether your product design architecture lets you get there.

## 6. Business Cases

### Anchor Case — BC-001: IKEA

**Company**: IKEA (Ingka Group + Inter IKEA Group)
**Pre-pivot state**: 85% of US-sold products imported; 15% domestic (down from 19% in 2014). Global flat-pack design = 10x container efficiency = structural import dependency. Tariff exposure on 85% of US revenue. [S01]
**Trigger**: October 14, 2025: 25% furniture tariffs on sofas, kitchen cabinets take effect. February 20, 2026: Supreme Court strikes IEEPA as unconstitutional. February 24, 2026: Section 122 flat 10-15% surcharge effective. Cumulative tariff: 35-40% on key categories. [S09]
**Structural Move**: (1) $70M SBA Home factory in Mocksville NC — 2M KALLAX units/year; (2) Sauder Woodworking partnership (Ohio); (3) 100% kitchen cabinet domestic sourcing completed; (4) Locus AI logistics acquisition for last-mile in-house (€100M/yr savings target); (5) Mattress domestic sourcing in evaluation. [S04] [S05] [S06]
**Economic Consequence**: Operating profit €1.7B (FY25) with "tariff costs partly absorbed." Prices raised October 2025: sofas +$50, bedroom sets +$100. Management publicly stated absorption limit reached. Margin compression confirmed in H2 FY25. [S03] [S08]
**Decision Implication**: IKEA's pivot proves the model: localize bulky, low-flat-pack items first (cabinets done); then mattresses; accept continued import dependency for high-efficiency flat-pack (KALLAX, BILLY). The question is not whether to localize — it's which categories cross the domestic break-even first.

---

### Counter Case — BC-002: Williams-Sonoma

**Company**: Williams-Sonoma (WSM)
**Pre-pivot state**: Premium furniture and homegoods retailer with historically higher domestic sourcing than mass-market competitors. US manufacturing long part of brand story ("Made in USA" labeling on select products). [S14]
**Trigger**: Same tariff environment as IKEA — but Williams-Sonoma entered the tariff era with a larger domestic sourcing base and a premium pricing model that more easily passes through cost increases. [S12]
**Structural Move**: Aggressive domestic sourcing investment pre-tariff escalation; vendor partnerships with US manufacturers for key categories; premium positioning allows price-pass-through that IKEA's "affordable" brand cannot execute as easily. [S14]
**Economic Consequence**: Williams-Sonoma maintained margins better than mass-market peers during the 2025 tariff escalation, partly due to higher domestic sourcing mix and partly due to premium consumer base with lower price sensitivity. [S14]
**Decision Implication**: Premium positioning + early domestic sourcing investment = tariff resilience. The counter-argument to IKEA's dilemma: you cannot solve a tariff problem by optimizing imports. The structural answer is domestic sourcing mix. Williams-Sonoma's early investment now looks prescient.

## 9. Debate Points

**Debate Point 1 — Does re-Americanization actually work for flat-pack at scale?**
*Pro (IKEA's public position)*: Kitchen cabinets fully localized; mattress sourcing under evaluation; NC factory proving automated production is viable at competitive quality. Domestic sourcing is a 10-year journey.
*Con (economic analysts + Home Furnishings Association)*: The flat-pack unit economics depend on volume thresholds that US factories cannot reach without decades of investment. 2M units/yr (Mocksville) vs. 20M+/yr (Chinese equivalent) means US production costs remain 40-60% higher even after tariff elimination. The NC factory is a PR signal, not a structural solution.
*Evidence split*: AMB Logistic [S01] vs. Home Furnishings Association [S14]

**Debate Point 2 — Should IKEA absorb tariffs or pass through?**
*Pro absorption*: IKEA's brand promise is democratic design + affordable prices. Passing costs through damages the brand positioning and risks losing the Millennial/Gen-Z core customer base (40%+ of visitors). CEO Brodin explicitly positioned affordability as the strategic priority at Davos [S07].
*Pro pass-through*: €1.7B operating profit [S03] on €41.5B revenue [S02] = 4.1% margin. The tariff absorption strategy reduces that margin further — at some point this becomes unsustainable. Williams-Sonoma's premium model shows that early price discipline plus domestic sourcing investment is more resilient than absorbing then raising.
*Evidence split*: Retail Dive / Axios [S08] vs. financial analyst commentary
