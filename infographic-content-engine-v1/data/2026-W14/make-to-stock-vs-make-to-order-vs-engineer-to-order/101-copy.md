## LinkedIn Caption

How does a car manufacturer decide to build 50,000 vehicles before a single buyer walks into a dealership? And why would a yacht builder refuse to start without a signed contract and a custom specification?
The answer is the production strategy.

This is Episode 7 of Supply Chain 101. In Episode 6 we covered push vs. pull and the decoupling point. Today we're zooming in on what that looks like in practice, because the production strategy is essentially where your company has placed that line.

There are three main strategies, and the simplest way to understand them is by asking: where does the customer order enter the process?

- Make to Stock (MTS) means you produce finished goods before any customer order exists. The product sits on a shelf or in a warehouse, ready to ship. Think cereal, toothpaste, or IKEA furniture. The advantage is immediate availability. The risk is that you're betting your forecast is right, and when it isn't, you're sitting on inventory nobody wants.

- Make to Order (MTO) means production only starts after a customer places an order. Think custom furniture, industrial equipment, or a Dell laptop circa 2005. The advantage is no finished goods inventory and a product tailored to the order. The trade-off is a longer lead time that the customer has to accept.

- Engineer to Order (ETO) means even the design doesn't start until the customer commits. Think Boeing aircraft, offshore oil platforms, or bespoke machinery. The advantage is a product built exactly to specification. The reality is lead times measured in months or years and a supply chain built entirely around project management.

Here's the thing most people miss: the mistake isn't choosing the wrong strategy. It's running one strategy across an entire portfolio when different product lines need different approaches. Your commodity products and your custom products should not be on the same planning logic.

Which strategy does your highest-margin product line use? And was that a deliberate choice?
