## Narrative Summary — Variant A (Paradox-Led)

Global agriculture built its nitrogen supply chain on one cost logic: source from where natural gas is cheapest. That logic concentrated 49% of global seaborne urea exports inside the Persian Gulf. On February 28, the world's cheapest fertilizer became the world's most stranded, exactly as the Northern Hemisphere opened its spring planting window.

### Why 49% of Nitrogen Comes From One Strait
- The natural gas advantage: Iran and Qatar combined hold roughly 40% of global proven gas reserves, making Gulf urea production 30–40% cheaper than in Europe or North America — the cost arbitrage that redirected global nitrogen trade to the Gulf over 30 years.
- The double blockade: Hormuz is not only blocking finished urea shipments; the simultaneous LNG halt has forced fertilizer plants in India, Pakistan, and Bangladesh to cut domestic production because they lack the gas feedstock to manufacture their own supply.
- Qatar's dual exposure: The world's largest LNG exporter is also a dominant urea and ammonia producer. Both products move through the same 2-mile shipping lane — one producer creating double chokepoint exposure that no other nitrogen supplier replicates.

### The 30-Day Clock Nobody Tracks
- The planting window: March/April is the critical nitrogen application period for corn, wheat, and soybean across the US Corn Belt, Western Europe, and Northern China — the three regions producing roughly 60% of global cereals.
- The biological rule: Agronomists confirm nitrogen must be applied within the first 30 days of plant growth or yield stunting is permanent. Late application does not recover stunted root and stem development. This is not supply chain flexibility. It is biology.
- The projection: Current 2026 models place a 12% loss in global cereal yields if the blockade persists through May — approximately 330 million tonnes of production that cannot be recovered by any June or July shipment.

### The Countries Already Running Out
- **49% of global seaborne nitrogen is stranded**: the supply that cannot be rerouted within biology's 30-day deadline. Alternative producers in Russia, China, and the US Gulf Coast exist but require 60–90 days to substitute at scale — exactly the window that closes before May.
- Brazil on 30%: The world's largest agricultural exporter imports 90–100% of its urea and has secured only 30% of inputs for its next crop cycle; May/June arrivals are classified unconfirmed across major trading house order books.
- India's June problem: The world's largest urea importer draws 49–50% of its supply from Gulf producers, and its Kharif season planting (June) requires 10 million tonnes currently trapped inside the Gulf with no confirmed replacement source.
