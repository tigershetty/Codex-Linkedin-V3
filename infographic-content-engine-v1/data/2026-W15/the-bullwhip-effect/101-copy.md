## LinkedIn Caption

A 5% increase in consumer demand can become a 40% spike at the raw material supplier!?!

Each link in the chain over-orders to protect itself, and the further upstream you go, the wilder the swings get. This has had a name since 1961 and most supply chains today are struggling to solve for it.

This is Episode 11 of Supply Chain 101. We're following the mechanics of how supply chains operate. Today's concept is the bullwhip effect, and once you see it, you'll recognise it everywhere.

Procter & Gamble first documented this with Pampers nappies. Retail sales were remarkably stable, but upstream orders were swinging wildly. The supply chain was reacting to orders, not to demand, and the gap between those two things is where the bullwhip lives.

There are four root causes, and they all share a common thread:
- Demand signal processing: each tier forecasts from orders, not actual demand
- Order batching: companies order in bulk at intervals rather than continuously
- Price fluctuations: promotions and discounts create artificial demand spikes
- Rationing and shortage gaming: when supply is tight, buyers inflate orders to secure allocation

What I believe is that this isn't a forecasting problem. It's an information-sharing problem. The fix isn't better algorithms. It's making actual demand data visible further upstream so every tier works from the same signal instead of amplifying it.

How many tiers up does your actual demand data travel?
