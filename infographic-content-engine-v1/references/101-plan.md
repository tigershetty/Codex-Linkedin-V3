# Shetty's Desk — Supply Chain 101: 90-Day Content Plan
**Version**: 2
**Used by**: `/101` skill (topic lookup)
**Period**: March – June 2026
**Format**: 24 single-image infographics, 2 per week, 12 weeks

---

## Series Logic

Each infographic builds on the previous one without requiring it. A follower who discovers the series at post 14 can understand it fully, but a follower from post 1 will see how concepts connect.

**Month 1 — The Language**: Shared vocabulary. What a supply chain is, how its functions, planning layers, and production strategies relate.
**Month 2 — The Mechanics**: How supply chains buy, hold inventory, manage cost, and move goods. The operating system.
**Month 3 — The Strategy**: Decisions that separate average supply chains from competitive advantages. Supply chain meets business performance.

## MONTH 1: THE LANGUAGE

| # | Topic | The Question It Answers | Hook | Visual Format | Caption Direction |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | What Is a Supply Chain? | If someone asks me at a dinner party, what do I actually say? | Your phone was 400 companies before it was yours. That chain of 400 is what I work on. | Single horizontal flow: Raw Material → Supplier → Manufacturer → Distributor → Retailer → Consumer. Six nodes, one line. | Open with the phone/400 companies line. Explain what a supply chain actually is in 2 sentences. Reframe: it is not a chain, it is a network. Close with: what product would surprise people most by its supply chain complexity? |
| 2 | Supply Chain vs. Logistics vs. Operations | My company uses these words interchangeably. Are they actually different? | Operations makes it. Logistics moves it. Supply chain decides whether any of it should happen in the first place. | Three-column comparison. Rows: Core question, Time horizon, Owns, Failure mode. 4 rows max. Clean grid, burnt orange header. | Open with the one-liner hook. Use coffee or a familiar product to trace which function handles which part. Three bullets: Operations = transformation, Logistics = movement, Supply chain = architecture. Close with: what does your company call the team that makes sourcing and planning decisions? |
| 3 | Planning vs. Scheduling | My manager says plan. My floor supervisor says schedule. Same thing? | Planning decides what to make next month. Scheduling decides what to make at 2pm on Tuesday. The gap between those two is where most factories lose money. | Two-panel split. Left: Planning (weeks to months, product families, capacity allocation). Right: Scheduling (hours to days, individual orders/machines, production sequence). Timeline bar showing where planning ends and scheduling begins. | Open with the hook. Planning = allocation and priorities across time. Scheduling = sequencing and timing on the floor. The mistake: plan well but schedule poorly = missed delivery dates. Close with: does your planning team talk to your scheduling team? |
| 4 | Strategic vs. Tactical vs. Operational Planning | Everyone says they do planning. But planning what, over what time frame? | Three levels of planning. Three time horizons. Three different people in the room. If your company treats them as one meeting, that is the problem. | Pyramid or stacked bars. Top: Strategic (1–5 years, network design, CEO/VP). Middle: Tactical (3–18 months, S&OP, Director). Bottom: Operational (0–3 months, scheduling, Planner). | Open with hook. Strategic: deciding to open a plant in Mexico. Tactical: capacity allocation to Product A vs B next quarter. Operational: which orders to prioritise today. Close with: which level does your team spend most time on? |
| 5 | What Is S&OP? | Everyone talks about S&OP. What actually happens in that room? | S&OP is a 5-step monthly meeting where sales and supply chain stop blaming each other and start agreeing on one number. Most companies run it. Few run it well. | 5-step flow: Demand Review → Supply Review → Pre-S&OP → Executive S&OP → Implementation. Each step: one-line description + who leads. | Open with hook. S&OP = bridge between commercial ambition and operational reality. The contrarian 30%: the meeting itself is not the point — the pre-work and accountability between meetings is. Close with: does your S&OP change behaviour, or is it a monthly PowerPoint ritual? |
| 6 | Push vs. Pull Supply Chains | My company pushes inventory into warehouses. My competitor seems to wait for orders. Who is right? | Push builds to forecast. Pull builds to demand. The line between them is called the decoupling point, and it is the most important decision in your supply chain you probably never made consciously. | Horizontal flow with vertical dotted line: Decoupling Point. Left (push): forecast-driven, inventory held, risk of overstock. Right (pull): demand-driven, responsive, risk of stockout. Examples: IKEA (push), Dell circa 2000 (pull). | Open with hook. Most supply chains are hybrid. Decoupling point = where forecast ends and demand response begins. Moving it upstream = more responsiveness but requires better demand visibility. Close with: where does the decoupling point sit in your supply chain? |
| 7 | Make to Stock vs. Make to Order vs. Engineer to Order | We hold finished goods inventory. Our competitor does not. Are we doing it wrong? | IKEA builds millions of Billy bookcases before anyone orders one. Boeing does not build a single 787 until an airline signs. Both are right. The difference is the production strategy. | Three horizontal swim lanes. MTS: production → stock → order → ship. MTO: order → production → ship. ETO: order → design → production → ship. Each shows where the customer order enters. | Open with IKEA/Boeing contrast. Choice depends on demand predictability, customisation, acceptable lead time. The mistake: running MTO product on MTS planning system. Close with: which strategy does your highest-margin product line use? |
| 8 | The SCOR Model in One Image | I keep hearing about SCOR. What is it and why should I care? | There are 250 supply chain metrics. SCOR organises them into 6 processes and 5 performance attributes. This is the framework that lets you compare any supply chain on earth to any other. | SCOR racetrack: Plan at centre, Source → Make → Deliver → Return around the loop, Enable as foundation. Right side: 5 performance attributes (Reliability, Responsiveness, Agility, Cost, Asset Management). | Open with hook. SCOR = common language. A SC manager at Unilever and one at Siemens can compare notes. 6 processes cover sourcing to returns. 5 attributes measure performance. Close with: if you had to pick one SCOR attribute your company is weakest on, which? |

## MONTH 2: THE MECHANICS

| # | Topic | The Question It Answers | Hook | Visual Format | Caption Direction |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| 9 | Purchasing vs. Procurement | Are these different roles or just different words? | Purchasing buys what someone asked for. Procurement decides whether they should be buying it at all, from whom, and under what terms. One is a transaction. The other is a strategy. | Two-column comparison. Left: Purchasing (reactive, transactional, short-term). Right: Procurement (proactive, strategic, long-term). Small diagram: procurement as larger circle containing purchasing. | Open with hook. Car-buying analogy: procurement = researching market, deciding features. Purchasing = negotiating price, signing papers. Close with: does your company have a procurement strategy, or just a purchasing department? |
| 10 | Direct vs. Indirect Procurement | We buy raw materials AND office supplies. Same team? | Direct procurement is what goes into your product. Indirect procurement is everything else. In most companies, indirect spend is 15–30% of revenue and almost nobody is managing it strategically. | Product at centre. Left arrows: Direct (raw materials, packaging, components). Bottom arrows: Indirect (facilities, IT, travel, MRO). Cost bar showing typical split. | Open with hook. Direct has always had strategic attention. Indirect historically fragmented. Contrarian: indirect is where easy savings are because nobody has been looking. Close with: who owns indirect spend in your organisation? |
| 11 | The Bullwhip Effect | Why does my supplier see wild demand swings when my retail sales are stable? | A 5% increase in consumer demand can become a 40% spike at the raw material supplier. This is the bullwhip effect, and it has been costing supply chains money since 1961. | Horizontal chain with 4 nodes. Above each: demand wave getting progressively larger upstream. Below: 4 root causes in 2×2 grid. | Open with hook. Reference P&G Pampers as origin. 4 causes. Reframe: not a forecasting problem, it is an information-sharing problem. Close with: how many tiers up does your actual demand data travel? |
| 12 | Safety Stock | How much extra inventory should I hold just in case? | Safety stock is the inventory you hope you never need but cannot afford not to have. Most companies either hold too much or calculate it with a formula from 1958 and never revisit. | Inventory sawtooth diagram: cycle stock, safety stock, reorder point, lead time. Below: 3 variables (demand variability, supply variability, service level). | Open with hook. Three inputs in plain language. The mistake: using average lead time instead of lead time variability. Contrarian: reducing lead time variability is a better lever than tweaking safety stock formulas. Close with: when did you last recalculate your safety stock parameters? |
| 13 | Lead Time: The Hidden Variable | My supplier says 6 weeks. Is that the only lead time I should care about? | There are at least 5 types of lead time in your supply chain. Your supplier quote covers one. The gap between what you measure and what you should measure is where delivery promises die. | Stacked bar/timeline: Information LT + Supplier LT + Manufacturing LT + Transportation LT + Receiving/QC LT = Cumulative LT. Different shades per segment. | Open with hook. Define each type in one sentence. Insight: cutting 3 days of decision time (information LT) often costs nothing and delivers as much value as cutting 3 days of transit time. Close with: have you mapped the full cumulative lead time for your top 10 SKUs? |
| 14 | ABC/XYZ Inventory Classification | I have 10,000 SKUs. How do I decide which ones get attention? | 20% of your SKUs drive 80% of your revenue. But revenue alone does not tell you how to plan them. The second dimension — demand predictability — changes everything. | 3×3 matrix. Rows: A/B/C (revenue). Columns: X/Y/Z (demand stability). Each box: short planning implication. Colour-coded green/amber/red. | Open with Pareto hook. ABC = value contribution, XYZ = demand pattern. AX = automate. AZ = manual attention. CZ = consider discontinuing. Close with: how many SKUs fall in CZ, and does anyone review them? |
| 15 | Total Cost of Ownership (TCO) | We chose the cheapest supplier. Why are we spending more? | Unit price is 30–50% of what you actually pay. The rest is invisible: freight, quality failures, payment terms, lead time buffers, inventory holding, admin overhead. That total is TCO. | Iceberg. Above waterline: Unit Price. Below: Transport, Customs, Quality costs, Inventory carrying, Admin, Lead time buffer, Supplier management. Below-waterline 2–3× larger visually. | Open with hook. One example: supplier 15% cheaper on unit price but 3× longer lead time requires 3× more safety stock, erasing the saving. Close with: does your procurement team evaluate on unit price or total cost? |
| 16 | 1PL → 2PL → 3PL → 4PL → 5PL | We use a 3PL. Our competitor uses a 4PL. What are we missing? | Five levels of logistics outsourcing. Each one trades control for capability. Most companies know they use a 3PL. Few know when to graduate to a 4PL. | 5-level staircase. Each step: PL level, one-line definition, example company, gain vs give up. 1PL (brewery delivering own beer) → 5PL (network-level, e-commerce platforms). | Open with hook. Walk up the ladder. Decision point: when complexity outgrows your team's ability to coordinate, that is the signal to move up. Close with: what level does your logistics operation actually require? |

## MONTH 3: THE STRATEGY

| # | Topic | The Question It Answers | Hook | Visual Format | Caption Direction |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| 17 | Demand Planning vs. Sensing vs. Shaping | We forecast demand. But our forecast is always wrong. Now what? | Demand planning predicts what customers will want. Demand sensing detects what they want right now. Demand shaping makes them want what you have. Three capabilities, most companies only do the first. | Three horizontal lanes on timeline. Planning (months ahead, 60–75% accuracy). Sensing (days to weeks, 80–90%). Shaping (proactive: pricing, promotions, substitution). | Open with hook. Forecasting will never be perfect. Sensing closes the gap short-term. Shaping is most underused: redirect demand instead of chasing it. Example: airline pricing = demand shaping. Close with: does your SC team influence pricing decisions? |
| 18 | Nearshoring vs. Offshoring vs. Reshoring | We manufacture in Asia. Should we move closer? | 80% of COOs plan to nearshore. 98% have not executed. The reason: they compared unit cost, not total landed cost. | World map with 3 zones + arrows. Below: 7-factor total landed cost checklist. Each factor shows directional shift across strategies. | Open with 80/98 stat. Unit cost comparison always favours offshoring. Add 6 other factors, math often flips. Zara example: nearshoring enabled margin advantage through speed. Close with: has your org run a total landed cost comparison? |
| 19 | Just-in-Time vs. Just-in-Case | Toyota proved JIT works. Then COVID proved it does not. Who is right? | JIT was the greatest SC innovation of the 20th century. JIC was the lesson of 2020. The answer is knowing which to apply where. | Two-panel split or balance scale. JIT vs JIC. Centre: spectrum showing most SCs sit between the two. Position determined by supply risk, demand variability, product criticality. | Open with hook. Toyota's original JIT required deep supplier partnerships, proximity, process reliability. Many copied low-inventory without the enabling conditions. Mature answer: segment your portfolio. Close with: which items run JIT by default rather than by design? |
| 20 | Single vs. Dual vs. Multi-Source | We use one supplier. Is that a risk or a strategy? | Single-sourcing gives leverage through volume. Multi-sourcing gives resilience through options. The decision is not about risk tolerance — it is about which risk you prefer to carry. | Three diagrams side by side. Below: decision matrix with 3 factors (supply risk, spend volume, criticality) and which strategy fits. | Open with hook. COVID triggered overcorrection from single to multi without considering trade-offs. Multi-sourcing a specialised component often increases cost without improving resilience if all suppliers are in the same geography. Close with: for your top 5 spend categories, can you name a tested alternative? |
| 21 | Supply Chain Risk Management | We got disrupted. Now leadership wants a risk framework. Where do we start? | Every supply chain disruption was predictable. Not the specific event — but the category. Companies that survived 2020–2025 had frameworks. The ones that scrambled had spreadsheets. | 2×2 risk matrix (Probability × Impact) with 5 risk categories plotted: Geopolitical, Supplier, Demand, Operational, External. Each with real 2020–2025 examples. | Open with hook. Walk through 5 categories with one example each. Reframe: risk management = reducing recovery time, not preventing disruption. Two levers: visibility and optionality. Close with: does your SC have a documented risk register? |
| 22 | Order-to-Cash vs. Procure-to-Pay | Finance mentions O2C and P2P. How do these connect to supply chain? | O2C is how money comes in. P2P is how money goes out. Supply chain sits in the middle of both. If you do not understand these cycles, you cannot explain your impact to a CFO. | Two stacked process flows. Top: O2C → DSO. Bottom: P2P → DPO. Arrows connecting where SC touches both. Gap = Cash Conversion Cycle. | Open with hook. O2C speed = how fast you collect. P2P efficiency = how smartly you spend. Combination = cash conversion cycle, the metric your CFO actually cares about. Close with: can you calculate your company's CCC? |
| 23 | The 10 Supply Chain KPIs That Matter | I measure 50 things. Which ones should I report to leadership? | You do not need 50 metrics. You need 10 that map to SCOR's 5 performance attributes. Two per attribute. One for the boardroom, one for the floor. | 5-row, 2-column grid. Rows = SCOR attributes. Column 1 = Strategic KPI (board). Column 2 = Operational KPI (floor). | Open with hook. SCOR provides structure. Boardroom KPI = is the SC healthy. Floor KPI = what to fix today. The mistake: reporting operational metrics to the board or giving strategic metrics to the warehouse. Close with: which of these 10 does your org track? |
| 24 | The Supply Chain Career Map | I want to grow in supply chain. What are the actual career paths? | Supply chain is not one career. It is at least 6, and they require different skills, different temperaments, and lead to different executive roles. This is the map nobody gives you. | Branching career map from Entry Level into 6 paths: Procurement, Planning/S&OP, Logistics, Manufacturing, Analytics, Strategy/Consulting. Each shows 3 role levels + C-suite destination. | Series finale. Reference that previous 23 concepts map to different career paths. Reframe: highest-impact leaders are T-shaped — deep in one, literate across all. Close with: which path are you on, and which adjacent path would make you most valuable? |
